\begin{abstract}
\begin{center}{\Large\textbf{
Trajectory Planning for Mission Completion Time Reduction in UAV-Assisted Data Collection in Wireless Sensor Networks
\vspace{0.3cm}\\
}\vspace{0.4cm}
}\end{center}

\begin{center}{\large 
Bo-Syun Ke\vspace{0.2cm} \\ 
Department of Electrical Engineering \vspace{0.2cm}\\ 
National Cheng Kung University \\
}\end{center}

\Thesisspace{\large 

    Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) provide a flexible solution for collecting data from ground nodes (GNs) in wireless sensor networks (WSNs), especially for GNs deployed in wide or hard-to-access areas. Mission completion time (MCT) is an important performance metric in UAV-assisted data collection tasks. Existing studies have mainly focused on reducing the UAV flight distance. The data collection process also affects the MCT because the transmission rate varies with the distance between the UAV and GNs.

    This thesis proposes a trajectory planning method for UAV-assisted WSN data collection. The proposed method first constructs a shortest geometric reference path that passes through the GN communication regions. This path provides global directional guidance for local trajectory planning. Based on this reference path, the Reference-Path Attractor (RPA) mechanism guides the selection of data collection trajectories for individual GNs according to their required data amounts. For consecutive GNs with overlapping communication regions, the Boundary-Overlap Strategy (BO) allows the UAV to switch service within the overlap region. This design reduces redundant boundary transitions between adjacent GNs. Simulation results show that the proposed method reduces the MCT compared with representative benchmark methods under different data requirements and GN distributions.
}

\emph{\textbf{Keywords}}---UAV, mission completion time, wireless sensor networks, trajectory planning, data collection.

\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}

    The Internet of Things (IoT) and wireless communication technologies have promoted the development of wireless sensor networks (WSNs). WSNs have been used in environmental monitoring, smart agriculture, post-disaster detection, military reconnaissance, and other sensing applications~\cite{Qin2019, Zhang2023, Xiang2023UAVMEC}. In these applications, ground nodes (GNs) observe physical environments and generate sensing data, such as temperature, humidity, air quality, and water quality. Each GN usually produces lightweight data packets~\cite{Meng2025UAVPathPlanningSurvey, Wei2022UAVIoTSurvey}. Since GNs are often limited by battery capacity, hardware cost, and transmission range, direct long-distance data transmission to a data center is difficult. Therefore, efficient data collection remains an important issue in WSNs.

    Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) provide an effective solution for data collection because of their mobility, flexible deployment, and favorable air-to-ground (A2G) line-of-sight (LoS) communication linkss~\cite{Zeng2019UAVTutorial, AlHourani2014LAPAltitude}. A UAV can move close to GNs and collect data when the UAV enters their communication regions. This approach enables data collection that was previously difficult to obtain using conventional methods~\cite{Zeng2019EnergyMinimization, Wu2018MultiUAVTrajectoryCommunication}.

    In UAV-assisted data collection tasks, mission completion time (MCT) is an important performance metric~\cite{Zhang2023CompletionTimeDRL}. MCT denotes the total time required for the UAV to depart from the data center, visit all target GNs to complete data collection, and return to the data center. For time-sensitive IoT applications, such as real-time forest fire monitoring and border intrusion detection, a shorter MCT allows the decision center to obtain field information earlier and to respond more quickly~\cite{Shen2022MultiUAVTimeWindows, Li2022MCT}. A shorter MCT also enables the UAV to serve more GNs within its limited battery~\cite{Li2023DataCollectionMaximization}.

    Many early studies simplified the MCT reduction problem by focusing mainly on UAV flight distance~\cite{Alemayehu2017TSPHeuristic}. This simplification will be insufficient if wireless communication conditions and data requirements are considered. A shorter geometric path can reduce flight time, but the data collection time also contributes to MCT. The transmission rate depends on the received signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). According to the Shannon formula, the transmission rate increases with the received SNR. In UAV-assisted data collection, a shorter distance between the UAV and a GN leads to a higher received SNR~\cite{Wang2022TrajectoryPower}. Therefore, a UAV that only follows a shortest geometric path can pass near the boundary of a GN communication region. In this case, the UAV reduces flight distance, but the larger communication distance can reduce the transmission rate and increase the data collection time. In contrast, a UAV that moves too close to a GN center can improve the transmission rate, but this movement could introduce additional flight distance. Therefore, MCT reduction requires a careful balance between flight distance and data collection time~\cite{Gong2018FlightTimeMinimization, Fu2024CollectionPoint}.

    The UAV only needs to enter the communication region of each GN to receive data and does not need to fly directly above every GN. Therefore, the task can be related to the Traveling Salesman Problem with Neighborhoods (TSPN), where each communication region is treated as a feasible service region)~\cite{Luo2021TrajectoryWSN}. Conventional TSPN mainly reduces the geometric path length. UAV-assisted data collection has an additional communication-related factor. The UAV must determine not only the visiting route among communication regions, but also the data collection trajectory inside each communication region.

    Existing studies have investigated UAV-assisted data collection from different directions. Some studies reduce the flight path through communication regions, while other studies adjust the data collection process inside a communication region~\cite{Luo2021TrajectoryWSN, Wang2020CollaborativeUAVs, He2024VShapedTrajectory}. These studies show that MCT is affected by both flight time and data collection time. Two issues still require further consideration. First, a local data collection trajectory should follow the overall flight direction of the mission. Without global geometric guidance, local trajectory adjustment can increase the total flight distance. Second, consecutive GNs can have overlapping communication regions. If the UAV switches service only at the boundary of each individual communication region, the UAV can perform redundant boundary transitions between adjacent GNs.

    Based on these observations, this thesis studies trajectory planning for UAV-assisted WSN data collection with one UAV. This thesis aims to reduce the MCT by considering the inter-region flight path, the intra-region data collection trajectory, and the service transition between consecutive GNs. The proposed method first constructs a shortest geometric reference path through the GN communication regions. This reference path provides global directional guidance for local trajectory planning. The proposed method then adjusts the data collection trajectory of each GN according to the required data amount. For consecutive GNs with overlapping communication regions, the proposed method allows the UAV to switch service within the shared coverage region. This design reduces unnecessary boundary transitions and keeps the trajectory closer to the overall flight direction.

    The main contributions of this thesis are summarized as follows.

    \begin{enumerate}
    	\item A trajectory planning method is developed for UAV-assisted WSN data collection. The method considers both the flight trajectory and the data collection trajectory to reduce the MCT.
    	\item A global-direction-guided local trajectory adjustment method is introduced. The method uses a shortest geometric reference path to guide the local data collection trajectory and maintain the UAV trajectory close to the shortest path.
    	\item A service switching strategy is developed for consecutive GNs with overlapping communication regions. The strategy allows the UAV to switch service within the shared coverage region and reduces redundant boundary transitions.
        \item Simulation results show that the proposed method reduces the overall MCT compared with benchmark methods under different data requirements and GN distributions.
    \end{enumerate}

    The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter 2 reviews related studies on trajectory planning for UAV-assisted WSN data collection. Chapter 3 introduces the system model and problem formulation. Chapter 4 describes the proposed trajectory planning method. Chapter 5 presents the simulation setup, and performance evaluation. Finally, Chapter~6 concludes this thesis and discusses future work

%------------------------- Related Works -------------------------%
\chapter{Related Work}\label{Chap:Related}
    
    Trajectory planning for UAV-assisted WSN data collection has been studied from several perspectives. This chapter reviews related studies from two main aspects. The first aspect is geometric path planning. This type of method treats each GN communication region as a feasible service region and shortens the UAV path that visits all regions. The second aspect is data collection trajectory design. This type of method considers the communication condition inside each region and adjusts the UAV movement according to the required data amount. These two aspects show that MCT is affected by both the flight path among communication regions and the data collection trajectory within each region.    

    UAV path planning has been modeled as a TSPN problem, and this problem has been shown to be NP-hard~\cite{Luo2021TrajectoryWSN}. A single-UAV-assisted data collection problem has also been studied with trajectory, altitude, velocity, and link scheduling~\cite{Li2020JointOptimization}. The study indicated that the UAV can support data transmission after entering the communication region of a ground user. The UAV does not have to fly directly above the ground user. The original mission time problem was transformed into a trajectory length problem. This design provides an important geometric path planning basis for UAV-assisted data collection. The primary objective is the reduction of geometric path length.

    Simply shortening the flight distance can place the UAV farther from the center of sensor nodes. A larger communication distance reduces the transmission rate and increases the time required for data reception~\cite{Gong2018FlightTimeMinimization}. These results show that flight distance, and communication rate are coupled in UAV data collection tasks. The location of the collection point affects the mission time in UAV-assisted data collection~\cite{Fu2024CollectionPoint}. The study showed that the UAV data collection point should not be selected only according to the shortest flight distance or the best communication quality. A point above a node provides better communication quality, but the UAV can spend more flight time to reach that point. A point near the communication boundary shortens the path, but the lower transmission rate increases the data collection time. Therefore, the selection of the collection point needs to consider both flight cost and communication condition.

    FM and HM have been introduced as two data collection modes for UAVs~\cite{Wang2020CollaborativeUAVs}. In FM, the UAV receives data while flying. This mode is suitable for nodes with smaller data requirements or better communication conditions. In HM, the UAV hovers at a specific location for data collection. This mode is suitable for nodes with larger data requirements or poorer communication conditions. The use of FM and HM allows the UAV to adjust the collection mode according to the node status and data requirement. The UAV does not need to serve all nodes with a fixed collection mode.

    For user scheduling and UAV trajectory design, the completion time problem for a single UAV collecting data from multiple sensor nodes has been investigated~\cite{Yuan2023CompletionTime}. Under specific conditions, a suitable UAV trajectory has an SHF structure. In this structure, the UAV hovers at selected locations for data collection and flies at the maximum speed between these hovering locations. The authors also emphasized the importance of segment-based scheduling. In this scheduling structure, the UAV mainly serves a specific sensor during a particular flying or hovering segment instead of frequently switching among different sensors.

    The effect of the data collection trajectory on the MCT was further investigated, and a V-shaped collection trajectory was proposed~\cite{Li2022MCT}. When the UAV moves into the interior of a communication region, the flight distance inside the region will increase. The UAV can also obtain a higher SNR and a higher transmission rate. This improvement can reduce the data collection time. The work also pointed out that the service trajectories of adjacent nodes are coupled. The previous node determines the starting position for serving the next node. If the service trajectory of each node is determined independently, the subsequent flight path will become longer. Therefore, reducing the data collection time of one node does not always reduce the overall MCT. The UAV needs to consider the continuous flight direction and the service connection between adjacent nodes.

    The above studies show that trajectory planning for UAV-assisted data collection mainly involves two design aspects. First, geometric path planning methods reduce the flight distance for the UAV to pass through multiple communication regions~\cite{Luo2021TrajectoryWSN, Li2020JointOptimization}. Second, data collection trajectory design indicates that the UAV can moderately move into the communication region according to the required data amount and communication conditions. This movement improves the transmission rate and reduces the data collection time~\cite{Li2022MCT, Fu2024CollectionPoint}. In addition, existing studies have explained the close relationship between UAV flight behavior and communication conditions from the perspectives of flight speed and transmit power control, as well as FM/HM mode switching~\cite{Gong2018FlightTimeMinimization, Wang2020CollaborativeUAVs, Yuan2023CompletionTime}.

    Based on the above research context, this thesis focuses on trajectory planning for single-UAV-assisted WSN data collection. Existing studies have shown the importance of geometric path shortening and data collection trajectory design. A method that mainly relies on geometric path length does not fully reflect the effect of UAV--GN distance variation on data collection time. A method that only adjusts the local data collection trajectory also needs a reference direction to maintain a short overall flight path. In addition, consecutive GNs could have overlapping communication regions. The overlapping region provides a feasible area for service switching. This thesis therefore considers the flight path among communication regions, the data collection trajectory inside each region, and the service transition between adjacent GNs. The objective is to reduce the overall MCT by maintaining a short flight path, improving data collection efficiency, and using overlapping regions for service switching.

\chapter{System Model}\label{Chap:Problem Formulation}

\section{System Model}

    As shown in Figure.~\ref{fig:system_model}, this thesis considers a UAV-assisted wireless sensor network in which a single UAV is employed to collect data from multiple ground nodes (GNs). The UAV departs from the data center, visits the communication regions of the GNs, collects the required data, and returns to the data center after the data collection task is completed. Let \(\mathcal{N}=\{1, 2,\ldots, N\}\) denote the set of GNs. The \(i\)-th GN is denoted by \(g_i\). A three-dimensional Cartesian coordinate system is adopted. In this coordinate system, the GNs are located on the ground plane, and the UAV flies at a fixed altitude \(H\). The elevation angle between the UAV and a GN is denoted by \(\theta\), and the maximum flying speed of the UAV is denoted by \(v_{\max}\).
    
    During the data collection process, the UAV operates in flying mode (FM) or hovering mode (HM). In FM, the UAV collects data while moving within the communication region of a GN. In HM, when the data collected along the flying trajectory is insufficient, the UAV hovers directly above the corresponding GN for additional data collection.
    
    \begin{figure*}[!t]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{./figures/system_model.png}
		\caption{Illustration of the UAV data collection process.} 
		\label{fig:system_model}
	\end{figure*}

    \noindent\textit{1) Transmission Model:} This thesis considers a delay-tolerant UAV-assisted WSN. Each GN (g_i) stores sensing data and uploads the data to the UAV when the UAV enters its communication region. Each GN is equipped with a single omnidirectional antenna and transmits with a fixed power \(P_t\) over a channel bandwidth \(B\). The uplink transmission rate from GN \(g_i\) to the UAV at time \(t\) is expressed as
    
    \begin{equation}
        R_i(t)=B\log_2\left(1+\gamma_i(t)\right)
    \end{equation}
    where \(\gamma_i(t)\) denotes the received signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) from GN \(g_i\) to the UAV at time \(t\). The SNR is given by
    \begin{equation}
        \gamma_i(t)=\frac{P_t}{\sigma^2 L_{dB_i}(t)}
    \end{equation}
    where \(\sigma^2\) denotes the noise power at the UAV receiver, and \(L_{dB_i}(t)\) represents the air-to-ground path loss between GN \(g_i\) and the UAV at time \(t\). The UAV can successfully collect data only when the received SNR satisfies \(\gamma_i(t)\geq\bar{\gamma}\). To avoid transmission interference among GNs, only the ground node being served is allowed to transmit data to the UAV during the corresponding service interval.


    \noindent\textit{2) Channel Model:} The UAV position changes during the mission, and the mission completion time is generally longer than the channel coherence time. Therefore, this thesis adopts a statistical channel model. The path loss from GN (g_i) to the UAV is expressed as
    \begin{equation}
        L_{dB_i}(t)=\left(\frac{4\pi f_c}{c}\right)^2 l_i^{\eta}\left[P_{\mathrm{LoS}_i}(t)\left(\xi_{\mathrm{LoS}}-\xi_{\mathrm{NLoS}}\right)+\xi_{\mathrm{NLoS}}\right]
    \end{equation}
    where \(f_c\) denotes the carrier frequency, \(c\) denotes the speed of light, and \(\eta\) denotes the path-loss exponent. The parameters \(\xi_{\mathrm{LoS}}\) and \(\xi_{\mathrm{NLoS}}\) represent the average additional path losses under LoS and NLoS propagation conditions, respectively. The three-dimensional distance between GN (g_i) and the UAV is denoted by (l_i(t)) and is given by
    \begin{equation}
        l_i(t)=\sqrt{\left(U_x(t)-GN_{xi}\right)^2+\left(U_y(t)-GN_{yi}\right)^2+H^2}
    \end{equation}
    where \(\left(GN_{xi},GN_{yi}\right)\) is the horizontal coordinate of GN \(g_i\), \(\left(U_x(t),U_y(t)\right)\) is the horizontal coordinate of the UAV at time \(t\), and \(H\) is the fixed flying altitude of the UAV. The LoS probability between GN \(g_i\) and the UAV is modeled as
    \begin{equation}
        P_{\mathrm{LoS}_i}(t)=\frac{1}{1+\alpha \exp\left[-\beta\left(\theta_i(t)-\alpha\right)\right]}
    \end{equation}
    where \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are parameters determined by the propagation environment. The elevation angle from GN \(g_i\) to the UAV at time \(t\) is denoted by \(\theta_i(t)\), which is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \theta_i(t)=\frac{180}{\pi}\arcsin\left(\frac{H}{l_i(t)}\right).
    \end{equation}

    Under this channel model, the path loss is affected by both the UAV--GN distance and the elevation angle. When the UAV moves closer to a GN, the three-dimensional distance decreases and the elevation angle increases, which generally improves the LoS probability and reduces the average path loss. Therefore, the UAV trajectory directly affects the received SNR and the corresponding data collection time.

\section{Problem Formulation}
    In the considered single-UAV data collection mission, all GNs must be served in one mission. The UAV starts from the data center, collects the required data from each GN, and returns to the data center. For a given transmit power \(P_t\), the communication radius of each GN is determined by the SNR threshold \(\bar{\gamma}\). Let \(D\) denote the maximum horizontal distance between the UAV and a GN such that the received SNR is equal to \(\bar{\gamma}\). When the UAV is located inside the communication region with radius \(D\), the received SNR is no less than the required threshold, and the UAV can successfully receive data from the corresponding GN. The value of \(D\) is obtained according to the adopted LoS-based air-to-ground channel model.
	
    \begin{figure*}[!t]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{./figures/uav_data_collection.png}
		\caption{UAV data collection process.} 
		\label{fig:uav_data_collection}
	\end{figure*}

    The MCT of the UAV is obtained by summing the service time of all GNs and the return time to the data center. For each GN, the service time consists of the flight time required to reach its communication region and the data collection time spent within that region. The index \(i\) follows the service sequence along the UAV trajectory. The GN visiting sequence is first determined by a genetic algorithm (GA). The proposed method then plans the complete UAV trajectory based on this sequence. As shown in Figure.~\ref{fig:uav_data_collection}, GN \(g_i\) is served independently, whereas GN \(g_{i+1}\) and GN \(g_{i+2}\) share an overlapping communication region.
	
    The service trajectory of GN \(g_i\) is described by three key points: the flying-in point \(\mathrm{FIP}_i\), the optimal hovering or turning point \(\mathrm{OH}_i\), and the flying-out point \(\mathrm{FOP}_i\). The point \(\mathrm{OH}_i\) has different meanings under FM and HM. In FM, \(\mathrm{OH}_i\) is the turning point of the V-shaped trajectory, and the UAV does not hover at this point. In HM, \(\mathrm{OH}_i\) is set to the GN center, and the UAV hovers at this point to collect the remaining data. The UAV first flies from the previous service point to \(\mathrm{FIP}_i\). This flight duration is denoted by \(t_{n_i}\). After entering the communication region of GN \(g_i\), the UAV collects data while moving from \(\mathrm{FIP}_i\) to \(\mathrm{FOP}_i\) through \(\mathrm{OH}_i\). The corresponding data collection time is denoted by \(t_{c_i}\).

    When the communication regions of two consecutive GNs do not overlap, the UAV enters and leaves the communication region of each GN through its own FIP and FOP, respectively. In this case, \(\mathrm{FIP}_i\) and \(\mathrm{FOP}_i\) are located on the boundary of the communication region with radius \(D\). The data collection trajectory of GN \(g_i\) is represented as
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{FIP}_i \rightarrow \mathrm{OH}_i \rightarrow \mathrm{FOP}_i.
    \end{equation}

    When the communication regions of GN \(g_{i+1}\) and GN \(g_{i+2}\) overlap, the service transition between the two GNs can be performed within the overlapping region. Therefore, the FOP of GN \(g_{i+1}\) and the FIP of GN \(g_{i+2}\) are not restricted to the boundaries of their respective communication regions. To describe this transition, this thesis defines an overlap switching point, denoted by \(P^{\mathrm{sw}}_{i+1,i+2}\). The switching point satisfies
    \begin{equation}
        P^{\mathrm{sw}}_{i+1,i+2} \in D_{i+1} \cap D_{i+2}
    \end{equation}
    where \(D_i\) denotes the communication region of GN \(g_i\). The switching point simultaneously serves as the FOP of GN \(g_{i+1}\) and the FIP of GN \(g_{i+2}\), which is expressed as
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{FOP}_{i+1}=P^{\mathrm{sw}}_{i+1,i+2}=\mathrm{FIP}_{i+2}.
    \end{equation}

    The service trajectory across the overlapping communication regions of GN \(g_{i+1}\) and GN \(g_{i+2}\) is represented as
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{FIP}_{i+1} \rightarrow \mathrm{OH}_{i+1} \rightarrow P^{\mathrm{sw}}_{i+1,i+2} \rightarrow \mathrm{OH}_{i+2} \rightarrow \mathrm{FOP}_{i+2}.
    \end{equation} 
    The UAV completes the data collection of GN \(g_{i+1}\) exactly upon reaching the switching point and begins serving GN \(g_{i+2}\) at the same point. This setting keeps the service trajectory continuous across the overlapping communication regions. For a group of consecutive overlapping GNs, an overlap switching point is defined between each adjacent GN pair in the group.

    Except for hovering-based data collection, the UAV flies at the maximum speed \(v_{\max}\). Therefore, the flight time is proportional to the corresponding flight distance. For the flight segment from the previous service point to \(\mathrm{FIP}_i\), the flight time \(t_{n_i}\) is expressed as
    \begin{equation}
        t_{n_i}=\frac{d_{i1}}{v_{\max}}
    \end{equation}
    where
    \begin{equation}
        d_{i1}=\left\|\mathrm{FIP}_i-\mathrm{FOP}_{i-1}\right\|.
    \end{equation}
    For \(i=1\), \(\mathrm{FOP}_0\) denotes the data center. If GN \(g_i\) and the previously served GN overlap, \(\mathrm{FIP}_i\) can be the corresponding overlap switching point. After completing the data collection task of the last GN, the UAV returns to the data center. The return time is given by
    \begin{equation}
        t^r=\frac{\left\|\mathrm{DC}-\mathrm{FOP}_N\right\|}{v_{\max}}
    \end{equation}
    where \(\mathrm{DC}\) denotes the data center.

    The data collection time of GN \(g_i\), denoted by \(t_{c_i}\), is evaluated according to the adopted V-shaped collection trajectory and the required data amount. If the data collected during flight is insufficient, the UAV hovers directly above GN \(g_i\) to collect the remaining data. Let \(Q_i\) denote the required data amount of GN \(g_i\). The accumulated received data during the collection interval must satisfy
    \begin{equation}
        \int_{0}^{t_{c_i}} R_i(t)\,dt \geq Q_i.
    \end{equation}
    The MCT of the UAV is expressed as
    \begin{equation}
        T=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(t_{c_i}+t_{n_i}\right)+t^r.
    \end{equation}

    Let \(\mathcal{P}\) denote the set of service points associated with the UAV trajectory. This set includes the FIPs, OHs, FOPs, and overlap switching points when communication regions overlap. Based on the above definitions, the MCT reduction problem is formulated as
    \begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:p1}
    \begin{align}
    \textbf{(P1):}\quad 
    &\min_{U(t),\mathcal{P}}
    \left[
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(t_{c_i}+t_{n_i}\right)+t^r
    \right] \notag \\
    \text{s.t.}\quad 
    &\gamma_i(t)\geq \bar{\gamma}, 
    \label{eq:p1_snr}\\
    &\mathrm{FIP}_i,\mathrm{OH}_i,\mathrm{FOP}_i \in D_i,
    \label{eq:p1_points}\\
    &P^{\mathrm{sw}}_{i,i+1} \in D_i \cap D_{i+1},\ i\in \mathcal{O},
    \label{eq:p1_overlap}\\
    &\int_{0}^{t_{c_i}} R_i(t)\,dt \geq Q_i.
    \label{eq:p1_data}
    \end{align}
    \end{subequations}

    Here, \(\mathcal{O}\) denotes the set of consecutive GN indices whose communication regions overlap, i.e., \(D_i \cap D_{i+1} \neq \emptyset\). Constraint~\eqref{eq:p1_snr} ensures that the received SNR satisfies the required threshold during data collection. Constraint~\eqref{eq:p1_points} requires the service points of each GN to lie within the corresponding communication region. Constraint~\eqref{eq:p1_overlap} defines the feasible region of the overlap switching point. Constraint~\eqref{eq:p1_data} guarantees that the UAV collects the required data amount from each GN.

   Problem \textbf{P1} is difficult to solve directly because the flight trajectory and the data collection trajectory affect each other. The selection of service points in \(\mathcal{P}\) affects both the flight time \(t_{n_i}\) and the data collection time \(t_{c_i}\). Moreover, when consecutive communication regions overlap, the overlap switching points affect the continuity of the service trajectory between adjacent GNs. Therefore, this thesis addresses \textbf{P1} through two trajectory planning components in the proposed solution: single-node trajectory planning and overlapping-node group trajectory planning. The RPA mechanism provides reference-path attractors for both components, whereas the BO strategy is specifically applied to overlapping-node groups to determine feasible service switching points within overlapping communication regions.

%------------------------- Proposed Solution -------------------------%
\chapter{Proposed Solution}\label{Chap:Proposed Solution}
	
    This chapter presents the proposed trajectory planning method for reducing the mission completion time (MCT) in UAV-assisted WSNs. Since the MCT is affected by both flight time and data collection time, the shortest geometric path does not necessarily lead to the shortest MCT. Therefore, the proposed method is developed based on two design considerations: reducing the flight distance and reducing the data collection time of each GN.

    To achieve this objective, this thesis first constructs a shortest geometric reference path to provide global directional information. Based on this reference path, the Reference-Path Attractor (RPA) mechanism is used to guide the subsequent local trajectory planning process. The final UAV trajectory is then determined according to the data collection requirement of each GN and the spatial relationship among communication regions. Through this design, the UAV trajectory can remain close to the shortest geometric reference path while allowing the UAV to move deeper into the communication region when such movement can improve the transmission rate and reduce the data collection time.

    Based on the spatial relationship among GNs, the proposed trajectory planning process is divided into two cases. For an independent GN whose communication region does not overlap with the communication regions of other GNs, this thesis applies single-node trajectory planning to determine the corresponding service trajectory. For consecutive GNs with overlapping communication regions, this thesis adopts the Boundary-Overlap Strategy (BO), which treats the overlapping GNs as a group and uses the flexibility provided by the overlapping regions to determine the service trajectory.

    The remainder of this chapter is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:4.1} introduces the construction of the shortest geometric reference path and the Reference-Path Attractor (RPA) mechanism. Section~\ref{sec:4.2} presents the single-node trajectory planning method. Section~\ref{sec:4.3} describes the overlapping-node group trajectory planning method based on BO.
    
\section{Reference Path Construction and RPA Mechanism}\label{sec:4.1}


    The purpose of constructing a shortest geometric reference path is to provide a global geometric direction for the subsequent trajectory planning process. If the trajectory segment associated with each GN is determined only according to the data collection time of the current GN, the selection of FIP and FOP lacks a global directional reference. As a result, the subsequent trajectory may deviate from the shortest geometric reference path, leading to an increase in flight distance. Therefore, this thesis first computes a shortest geometric reference path that passes through all GN communication regions and then extracts reference-path attractors from this path. In this thesis, an attractor is defined as a reference point extracted from the shortest geometric reference path within the communication region of a GN. The attractor is used as a directional reference when evaluating candidate FIP/FOP pairs in later trajectory planning. The UAV is not required to pass through the attractor.

    The service order defined in the problem formulation is used in this section. The index \(i\) denotes the \(i\)-th GN visited by the UAV, and the communication region of this GN is denoted by \(\mathcal{D}_i\), as defined in the system model. For the given visiting sequence, this thesis computes a shortest geometric reference path that passes through all communication regions \(\mathcal{D}_i\). To formulate the reference path construction problem, two auxiliary reference-path points are introduced for each communication region. Let \(\mathbf{r}_i^{\mathrm{in}}\) and \(\mathbf{r}_i^{\mathrm{out}}\) denote the reference-path entry point and reference-path exit point associated with \(\mathcal{D}_i\), respectively. These two points are used only for constructing the shortest geometric reference path and are not the final FIP and FOP used for data collection. The shortest geometric reference path is obtained by solving
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:reference-path-construction}
    \begin{aligned}
    \min_{\{\mathbf{r}_i^{\mathrm{in}},\mathbf{r}_i^{\mathrm{out}}\}_{i=1}^{N}} \quad
    & \left\|\mathbf{r}_1^{\mathrm{in}}-\mathbf{q}_0\right\|
    + \sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\mathbf{r}_i^{\mathrm{out}}-\mathbf{r}_i^{\mathrm{in}}\right\| \\
    &+ \sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\left\|\mathbf{r}_{i+1}^{\mathrm{in}}-\mathbf{r}_i^{\mathrm{out}}\right\|
    + \left\|\mathbf{q}_0-\mathbf{r}_N^{\mathrm{out}}\right\| \\
    \text{s.t.} \quad
    & \mathbf{r}_i^{\mathrm{in}} \in \mathcal{D}_i \\
    & \mathbf{r}_i^{\mathrm{out}} \in \mathcal{D}_i .
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    where \(\mathbf{q}_0\) denotes the data center. The first term represents the distance from the data center to the reference-path entry point of the first visited GN. The second term represents the geometric segment inside each communication region. The third term represents the distance from the reference-path exit point of the current GN to the reference-path entry point of the next visited GN. The last term represents the return distance from the last communication region to the data center. Since the objective function is composed of Euclidean norm terms and the feasible communication regions are convex, \eqref{eq:reference-path-construction} can be formulated as a SOCP problem.

    The segment from \(\mathbf{r}_i^{\mathrm{in}}\) to \(\mathbf{r}_i^{\mathrm{out}}\) represents the portion of the shortest geometric reference path inside the communication region \(\mathcal{D}_i\). This segment is not the actual data collection trajectory of the (i)-th GN. The final FIP, OH, and FOP are determined later according to the data requirement of the GN and the corresponding data collection time. The shortest geometric reference path provides geometric reference information rather than the final UAV trajectory for the subsequent trajectory planning process.


    After the shortest geometric reference path is obtained, the RPA mechanism extracts one attractor for each GN. Let the shortest geometric reference path be denoted by \(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ref}}\). The path \(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is scanned according to the traveling direction from the data center. For the \(i\)-th GN, the candidate attractor points are the points on \(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ref}}\) that lie within the communication region \(\mathcal{D}_i\). Let \(\ell(\mathbf{p})\) denote the cumulative path length from \(\mathbf{q}_0\) to point \(\mathbf{p}\) along \(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ref}}\). The attractor of the \(i\)-th GN is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{a}_i=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ref}}\cap\mathcal{D}_i}\ell(\mathbf{p}).
    \end{equation}
    The reference path used by the RPA mechanism provides a reference direction for UAV flight. In this thesis, the shortest geometric reference path is selected because the main objective is to reduce the MCT, and the flight distance directly affects the flight time. The RPA mechanism itself is not limited to the shortest geometric reference path. If another path is selected as the reference path, the attractors can still be defined based on that path. For example, the environment can contain buildings, mountains, forests, or power towers, and the UAV cannot fly directly through these obstacles. To avoid obstacles, an obstacle-avoidance path can be used as the reference path. In addition, airports, military areas, densely populated areas, or regulations can create flight-restricted regions. To avoid these flight-restricted regions, a no-fly-zone-constrained path can be used as the reference path. In these cases, the attractors provide directional references derived from the selected reference path. The local trajectory planning process can then adjust the entry and exit points according to both the selected global direction and the data collection requirement.

    As shown in Figure.~\ref{fig:reference_path_attractors}, the shortest geometric reference path passes through the communication regions of the GNs according to the visiting direction. The triangular markers indicate the reference-path attractors extracted from this path. Each attractor is selected from the portion of the shortest geometric reference path that lies within the corresponding communication region.
    \begin{figure*}[!t]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{./figures/reference_path_attractors.png}
		\caption{Illustration of the reference-path attractors.} 
		\label{fig:reference_path_attractors}
	\end{figure*}

    The attractor \(\mathbf{a}_i\) is selected as the first reference-path point that lies within the communication region of the \(i\)-th GN along the traveling direction. The terminal attractor is set as the data center, \(\mathbf{a}_{N+1}=\mathbf{q}_0\).

    The influence of the attractor can be explained by decomposing the flight-time term associated with the \(i\)-th GN. Let \(\mathrm{P}_i^{\mathrm{pre}}\) denote the point immediately before serving the \(i\)-th GN. The point \(\mathrm{P}_i^{\mathrm{pre}}\) can be the FOP of the previous GN, an overlap switching point, or the data center when \(i=1\). For a candidate \(\mathrm{FIP}_i\), the fly-in time is expressed as
    \begin{equation}
        t_{n_i}^{\mathrm{in}}=\frac{\left\|\mathrm{FIP}_i-\mathrm{P}_i^{\mathrm{pre}}\right\|}{v_{\max}}.
    \end{equation}
    After the data collection process of the \(i\)-th GN is completed, the outgoing flight direction is evaluated with respect to the next attractor \(\mathbf{a}_{i+1}\). The RPA-guided fly-out time is expressed as
    \begin{equation}
        t_{n_i}^{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{\left\|\mathbf{a}_{i+1}-\mathrm{FOP}_i\right\|}{v_{\max}}.
    \end{equation}
    Accordingly, the flight-time term considered in the RPA-guided trajectory planning process is written as
    \begin{equation}
        t_{n_i}^{\mathrm{RPA}}=t_{n_i}^{\mathrm{in}}+t_{n_i}^{\mathrm{out}}.
    \end{equation}
    The fly-out term \(t_{n_i}^{\mathrm{out}}\) shows how the next attractor affects the evaluation of the current FOP. When different FIP/FOP pairs are considered in the trajectory evaluation process, the selected FOP determines the flight time toward the next attractor. Therefore, the RPA mechanism affects the selection of the FIP/FOP pair through the RPA-guided flight-time term.

    A candidate FIP/FOP pair is evaluated according to both the data collection time of the current GN and the flight time toward the reference direction derived from the shortest geometric reference path. The UAV trajectory is not constrained to pass through the attractor. The complete candidate evaluation rule is described in Section~\ref{sec:4.2}.

    Algorithm~\ref{alg:rpa} summarizes the procedure for constructing the shortest geometric reference path and extracting the corresponding reference-path attractors. Based on the extracted attractors and the RPA-guided flight-time term, Section~\ref{sec:4.2} presents the single-node trajectory planning method, including the generation of candidate FIP/FOP pairs, the determination of OH, and the evaluation of \(t_{c_i}\). Section~\ref{sec:4.3} further describes how the trajectory planning process is modified when consecutive GNs have overlapping communication regions.

    \begin{algorithm}[!t]
    \small
    \caption{Reference Path Construction and Attractor Extraction}
    \label{alg:rpa}
    \KwIn{Communication regions \(\{\mathcal{D}_i\}_{i=1}^{N}\) in the visiting order; data center \(\mathbf{q}_0\)}
    \KwOut{Shortest geometric reference path \(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ref}}\); reference-path attractors \(\{\mathbf{a}_i\}_{i=1}^{N+1}\)}

    Solve the SOCP problem in \eqref{eq:reference-path-construction} to obtain \(\{\mathbf{r}_i^{\mathrm{in}},\mathbf{r}_i^{\mathrm{out}}\}_{i=1}^{N}\)\;

    Construct \(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ref}}\) by connecting \(\mathbf{q}_0\), \(\mathbf{r}_1^{\mathrm{in}}\), \(\mathbf{r}_1^{\mathrm{out}}\), \(\ldots\), \(\mathbf{r}_N^{\mathrm{in}}\), \(\mathbf{r}_N^{\mathrm{out}}\), and \(\mathbf{q}_0\) in sequence\;

    Define \(\ell(\mathbf{p})\) as the cumulative path length from \(\mathbf{q}_0\) to point \(\mathbf{p}\) along \(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ref}}\)\;

    \For{\(i=1\) \KwTo \(N\)}{
        Extract the first reference-path point that lies within \(\mathcal{D}_i\)\;
    
        Set \(\mathbf{a}_i \gets \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ref}}\cap\mathcal{D}_i}\ell(\mathbf{p})\)\;
    }

    Set the terminal attractor as \(\mathbf{a}_{N+1}\gets \mathbf{q}_0\)\;

    \Return \(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ref}}\) and \(\{\mathbf{a}_i\}_{i=1}^{N+1}\)\;
    \end{algorithm}


\section{Single-Node Trajectory Planning}\label{sec:4.2}

    This section describes the trajectory planning process for an independent GN. An independent GN is defined as a GN whose communication region does not overlap with the communication regions of other GNs. Based on the RPA-guided flight-time term defined in Section~\ref{sec:4.1}, this planning process selects a suitable FIP/FOP pair while considering the data collection time inside the communication region. The corresponding OH is determined during the evaluation of the V-shaped data collection trajectory.

    \begin{figure*}[!t]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{./figures/single_node_data_collection.png}
		\caption{V-shaped trajectory for single-node data collection.} 
		\label{fig:single_node_data_collection}
	\end{figure*}

    As shown in Figure.~\ref{fig:single_node_data_collection}, the UAV collects data while moving inside the communication region of the GN. Candidate FIP and FOP points are generated on the communication boundary of the GN. Let \(\mathcal{F}_i^{\mathrm{in}}\) and \(\mathcal{F}_i^{\mathrm{out}}\) denote the candidate sets of FIP and FOP, respectively. For each candidate pair \((\mathrm{FIP}_i,\mathrm{FOP}_i)\), the corresponding \(\mathrm{OH}_i\) and the data collection time \(t_{c_i}\) are evaluated according to the required data amount \(C_i\).

    For each candidate pair, the corresponding V-shaped trajectory is evaluated to determine whether the required data amount can be collected during flight. If the flying collection is sufficient, FM is adopted. In this case, \(\mathrm{OH}_i\) is only the turning point of the V-shaped trajectory, and the UAV keeps flying through this point. If the flying collection is insufficient, HM is adopted. In HM, \(\mathrm{OH}_i\) is set to the GN center, and the UAV hovers at this point to collect the remaining data. After the data collection time is obtained for all candidate pairs, the candidate pair is selected by considering both \(t_{c_i}\) and the RPA-guided flight-time term \(t_{n_i}^{\mathrm{RPA}}\).

    For a given FIP/FOP pair, the flying distance inside the communication region is determined by the sum of the distance from FIP to OH and the distance from OH to FOP. According to the geometric property of an ellipse, all points with the same sum of distances to \(\mathrm{FIP}_i\) and \(\mathrm{FOP}_i\) form an elliptical curve. Therefore, different V-shaped trajectories can be evaluated by adjusting \(\mathrm{OH}_i\) and the corresponding internal flying distance. When the required data amount can be collected during flight, a bisection search is used to determine a feasible V-shaped flying trajectory that satisfies the data collection requirement.
    
    The amount of data collected during flight is calculated by integrating the instantaneous transmission rate along the trajectory:
    \begin{equation}
        C_i^{\mathrm{FM}}=\int_{0}^{t_i^{\mathrm{FM}}} R_i(t)\,dt.
    \end{equation}
    where \(t_i^{\mathrm{FM}}\) denotes the flying collection time of the candidate V-shaped trajectory. If \(C_i^{\mathrm{FM}}\geq C_i\), the required data amount can be collected in FM, and the data collection time is given by \(t_{c_i}=t_i^{\mathrm{FM}}\). If \(C_i^{\mathrm{FM}}<C_i\), the flying trajectory cannot collect all required data. The UAV then operates in HM. In HM, OH is used as the hovering point, and the UAV collects the remaining data at this point. The additional hovering time is calculated as
    \begin{equation}
        t_i^{\mathrm{H}}=\frac{C_i-C_i^{\mathrm{FM}}}{R_i(\mathrm{OH}_i)}.
    \end{equation}
    Therefore, the data collection time in HM is
    \begin{equation}
        t_{c_i}=t_i^{\mathrm{FM}}+t_i^{\mathrm{H}}.
    \end{equation}
    After \(t_{c_i}\) is obtained for all candidate FIP/FOP pairs, each candidate is evaluated together with the RPA-guided flight-time term. The selected pair is determined by
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:min-fip-fop}
        \min_{\mathrm{FIP}_i,\mathrm{FOP}_i}
        \ t_{c_i}\left(\mathrm{FIP}_i,\mathrm{OH}_i,\mathrm{FOP}_i\right)
        + t_{n_i}^{\mathrm{RPA}} .
    \end{equation}

    In \eqref{eq:min-fip-fop}, \(\mathrm{OH}_i\) is determined during the evaluation of \(t_{c_i}\). Therefore, the selected pair is determined by considering both the data collection time inside the communication region and the RPA-guided flight time. After the trajectory of the current GN is determined, the resulting FOP is used as the starting point for planning the next GN. When consecutive GNs have overlapping communication regions, the corresponding overlapping-node trajectory planning method is described in Section~\ref{sec:4.3}.


\section{Overlapping-Node Group Trajectory Planning}\label{sec:4.3}

    This section describes the trajectory planning method for GNs with overlapping communication regions. For an independent GN, the FIP and FOP candidates are generated on the communication boundary of a single GN, as described in Section~\ref{sec:4.2}. When the communication regions of adjacent GNs overlap, the overlapping region provides additional flexibility for service transition. If the UAV is still forced to leave one communication region from its boundary and enter the next communication region from another boundary point, unnecessary flight distance may be introduced. 

    To use the overlapping region between adjacent GNs, this thesis proposes the Boundary-Overlap Strategy (BO). BO allows the switching point \(P^{\mathrm{sw}}_{i,i+1}\), defined in the system model, to be selected from the overlapping region of \(g_i\) and \(g_{i+1}\). In this way, the UAV can connect the service trajectory of two adjacent GNs through the overlapping region, instead of performing an additional boundary-to-boundary transition.

    \begin{figure*}[!t]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{./figures/overlap_trajectory.png}
		\caption{Boundary-Overlap Strategy for overlapping GNs.} 
		\label{fig:overlap_trajectory}
	\end{figure*}

    As shown in Figure.~\ref{fig:overlap_trajectory}, multiple adjacent GNs with overlapping communication regions are considered as one group. Let
    \begin{equation}
        G_m=\{g_i,g_{i+1},\ldots,g_j\}
    \end{equation}
    denote the \(m\)-th overlapping-node group, where \(g_i\) and \(g_j\) are the first and last GNs in the group, respectively. Here, \(G_m\) denotes a set of GNs, while \(g_i\) denotes an individual GN. For any two adjacent GNs \(g_k\) and \(g_{k+1}\) in \(G_m\), where \(k=i,\ldots,j-1\), their communication regions overlap. The UAV enters the group from the FIP of \(g_i\), passes through the switching points in the overlapping regions and leaves the group from the FOP of \(g_j\). For example, if the group contains \(g_i\), \(g_{i+1}\), and \(g_{i+2}\), the service trajectory inside the group can be represented as
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{FIP}_i
        \rightarrow
        \mathrm{OH}_i
        \rightarrow
        P^{\mathrm{sw}}_{i,i+1}
        \rightarrow
        \mathrm{OH}_{i+1}
        \rightarrow
        P^{\mathrm{sw}}_{i+1,i+2}
        \rightarrow
        \mathrm{OH}_{i+2}
        \rightarrow
        \mathrm{FOP}_{i+2}.
    \end{equation}
    In this sequence, \(\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{sw}}_{i,i+1}\) is used as the exit point of \(g_i\) and the entry point of \(g_{i+1}\). Similarly, \(\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{sw}}_{i+1,i+2}\) connects the service trajectories of \(g_{i+1}\) and \(g_{i+2}\). Therefore, the group is planned as a single service unit with one entry point and one exit point.

    For each overlapping region, BO selects the switching point from a finite candidate set instead of searching over the entire continuous overlapping region. The candidate set is generated using two representative sampling directions. The first direction follows the line segment connecting the centers of the two adjacent GNs. Different adjacent GN pairs can have different overlap depths. BO provides switching candidates at different positions within the overlapping region and adjusts the service transition position accordingly. The second direction follows a perpendicular sampling line inside the overlapping region. This direction provides candidates with different inward depths within the overlapping region. Since different data amounts may require different collection depths, the perpendicular sampling line allows BO to adjust the switching location according to the data collection requirement. By combining these two representative directions, the T-shaped sampling method generates switching candidates for the overlapping region without searching all possible points in the continuous region.

    For the \(m\)-th overlapping-node group \(G_m\), a candidate point sequence is written as
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{S}_m=
        \left[
        \mathrm{FIP}_i,
        \mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{sw}}_{i,i+1},
        \ldots,
        \mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{sw}}_{j-1,j},
        \mathrm{FOP}_j
        \right].
    \end{equation}

    The first point in \(\mathcal{S}_m\) is the group entry point, and the last point is the group exit point. The intermediate points are switching points selected from the overlapping regions. Given a candidate sequence, each GN uses the previous point in the sequence as its entry point and the next point as its exit point. After a candidate sequence \(\mathcal{S}_m\) is determined, the data collection time of each GN in the group is evaluated according to the V-shaped trajectory model described in Section~\ref{sec:4.2}. The collection-time term of the group is defined as the sum of the data collection times of all GNs in the group:
    \begin{equation}
        t_{c,m}=\sum_{k=i}^{j}t_{c_k}
    \end{equation}
    In addition to the collection-time term, the flight time before entering and after leaving the group must also be considered. Since the overlapping-node group is served as one continuous trajectory segment, the group has one entry point and one exit point. The flight-time term of the group is expressed as
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:time-tn_m}
        t_{n,m}
        =
        \frac{\left\|\mathrm{FIP}_i-\mathrm{FOP}_{i-1}\right\|}{v_{\max}}
        +
        \frac{\left\|\mathbf{a}_{j+1}-\mathrm{FOP}_j\right\|}{v_{\max}}.
    \end{equation}

    Therefore, each candidate sequence is evaluated by
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:time-T_m}
        T_m=t_{c,m}+t_{n,m}.
    \end{equation}

    \begin{algorithm}[!t]
    \small
    \caption{Boundary-Overlap Strategy for an Overlapping-Node Group}
    \label{alg:bo}
    \KwIn{Overlapping-node group \(G_m=\{g_i,g_{i+1},\ldots,g_j\}\); communication regions \(\{\mathcal{D}_k\}_{k=i}^{j}\); previous service point \(\mathrm{FOP}_{i-1}\); next attractor \(\mathbf{a}_{j+1}\)}
    \KwOut{Selected candidate sequence \(\mathcal{S}_m\) and evaluation time \(T_m\)}

    Generate the candidate FIP set of \(g_i\) and set it as \(\mathcal{L}_0\)\;

    \For{\(k=i\) \KwTo \(j-1\)}{
        Generate switching candidates in \(\mathcal{D}_k \cap \mathcal{D}_{k+1}\) by the T-shaped sampling method\;
        Set the generated candidates as layer \(\mathcal{L}_{k-i+1}\)\;
    }

    Generate the candidate FOP set of \(g_j\) and set it as \(\mathcal{L}_{j-i+1}\)\;

    \If{\(g_i\) is the first GN in the visiting order}{
        Set \(\mathrm{FOP}_{i-1}\gets \mathbf{q}_0\)\;
    }

    \ForEach{\(p\in\mathcal{L}_0\)}{
        Set \(\Phi_0(p)\gets \left\|p-\mathrm{FOP}_{i-1}\right\|/v_{\max}\)\;
        Set \(\mathrm{Pred}_0(p)\gets \emptyset\)\;
    }

    \For{\(r=0\) \KwTo \(j-i\)}{
        Set \(k=i+r\)\;
        \ForEach{\(q\in\mathcal{L}_{r+1}\)}{
            Compute \(\Phi_{r+1}(q)\) by selecting the predecessor \(p\in\mathcal{L}_r\) with the smallest accumulated evaluation time\;
            Store the corresponding predecessor as \(\mathrm{Pred}_{r+1}(q)\)\;
        }
    }

    Select \(p^{\mathrm{end}}\in\mathcal{L}_{j-i+1}\) with the smallest value of
    \(\Phi_{j-i+1}(p)+\left\|\mathbf{a}_{j+1}-p\right\|/v_{\max}\)\;

    Compute \(T_m\) according to the selected \(p^{\mathrm{end}}\)\;

    Backtrack from \(p^{\mathrm{end}}\) using the stored predecessors to obtain \(\mathcal{S}_m\)\;

    \Return \(\mathcal{S}_m\) and \(T_m\)\;
    \end{algorithm}

    In \eqref{eq:time-T_m}, \(T_m\) denotes the evaluation time of a candidate sequence for the \(m\)-th overlapping-node group. The term \(t_{c,m}\) represents the total data collection time of the GNs in the group, while \(t_{n,m}\) represents the flight time associated with entering and leaving the group. The candidate sequence with the smallest \(T_m\) is selected as the service trajectory of the group. In \eqref{eq:time-tn_m}, the first term represents the flight time from the FOP of the previous GN to the entry point of the group, and the second term represents the flight time from the exit point of the group toward the next attractor.

    Since a group may contain more than two GNs, directly enumerating all possible combinations of entry points, switching points, and exit points may increase the search cost. BO therefore organizes the candidate points into layers. The first layer contains candidate FIP points of the first GN in the group, the intermediate layers contain candidate switching points generated in the overlapping regions, and the last layer contains candidate FOP points of the last GN. A dynamic programming search is then applied over these layers to find the candidate sequence with the smallest accumulated evaluation time.

    After the trajectory of the group is determined, the selected \(\mathrm{FOP}_j\) is used as the previous service point for the next planning step. When the next GN is independent, the single-node trajectory planning method in Section~\ref{sec:4.2} is applied. When another overlapping-node group is encountered, BO is applied again. The detailed procedure of BO is summarized in Algorithm~2.

\chapter{Performance Evaluation}\label{Chap:Implementation}

    This chapter evaluates the performance of the proposed Reference-Path Attractor and Boundary-Overlap (RPA-BO) scheme for UAV-assisted WSN data collection. The main objective of the evaluation is to examine whether the proposed scheme can reduce the mission completion time (MCT) under different data sizes. In addition to MCT, the total flight distance is also evaluated, since the flight distance directly affects the UAV flight time and also reflects how different trajectory planning schemes guide the UAV through the GN communication regions.
    
    Three schemes are compared in the simulation. The first scheme is the Convex shortest path, which computes the shortest geometric path passing through the communication region of each GN. This scheme is used as a geometric path baseline because its main purpose is to reduce the flight distance through the GN communication regions. The second scheme is the V-shaped scheme, which adopts the V-shaped data collection trajectory. This scheme considers the trajectory inside the communication region of each GN and adjusts the UAV service trajectory according to the data amount. The third scheme is the proposed RPA-BO scheme. This scheme uses reference-path attractors to provide global geometric guidance during local trajectory planning and applies the Boundary-Overlap Strategy to handle consecutive overlapping GN communication regions. When adjacent communication regions overlap, the UAV can select a switching point inside the overlapping region instead of leaving one communication boundary and entering another. For a fair comparison, the three schemes are evaluated under the same GN deployment and the same visiting order in each simulation run. Therefore, the differences in MCT and flight distance mainly result from the trajectory planning and data collection strategy adopted by each scheme.

The UAV departs from the data center, visits all GNs according to the generated visiting order, collects the required data from each GN, and returns to the data center after completing the mission. For each data size, 200 independent simulation runs are conducted with different random GN deployments. The reported MCT and flight distance results are the average values over these 200 runs. The required data amount of each GN is denoted by \(C\). The data amount is set to \(C\in\{1,2,4,8,16,32\}~\mathrm{MB}\). Table~\ref{tab:simulation-parameters} lists the main simulation parameters used in the performance evaluation.

    Figure.~\ref{fig:final_trajectories} compares the final trajectories of the proposed RPA-BO scheme, Convex shortest path, and V-shaped scheme under the \(8~\mathrm{MB}\) data size. In this case, the Convex shortest path has the shortest flight distance of \(4407.78~\mathrm{m}\), but its MCT is \(488.59~\mathrm{s}\). This result indicates that a shorter geometric flight distance does not necessarily lead to a shorter MCT, because the UAV may collect data near the communication boundary, where the transmission rate is relatively low. The V-shaped scheme reduces the MCT to \(341.12~\mathrm{s}\) by moving the UAV deeper into GN communication regions. This movement improves the transmission rate during data collection, but the flight distance increases to \(6766.34~\mathrm{m}\). The proposed RPA-BO scheme achieves the lowest MCT of \(303.40~\mathrm{s}\) with a flight distance of \(6053.94~\mathrm{m}\). Compared with the V-shaped scheme, the proposed scheme reduces the MCT while keeping a shorter flight distance.

    This improvement mainly comes from two aspects. First, unlike schemes that restrict the entry and exit points to the boundaries of communication regions, the BO strategy optimizes the service switching locations within overlapping communication regions. As a result, after completing data collection from one GN, the UAV can immediately switch to serving the next GN within the overlap area, without first leaving the communication region of the current GN and then entering that of the next GN. This switching strategy reduces unnecessary flight distance and redundant movement between neighboring communication regions. Second, the RPA mechanism provides global geometric guidance during local trajectory planning, so the UAV trajectory can remain closer to the global geometric direction while still considering the data collection requirement. Therefore, the proposed RPA-BO scheme can reduce the flight distance and shorten the mission completion time.

Even when the required data amount is small, selecting appropriate data collection trajectories and overlap switching points can reduce the time spent on service transitions and data collection, thereby leading to a shorter overall MCT.

The proposed scheme further reduces the MCT through reference-path guidance and overlap-aware switching.

This result indicates that the proposed scheme can reduce the flight distance while maintaining the benefits of efficient data collection.

\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work} \label{Chap:Conclusion}

\section{Conclusion}

    This thesis studied trajectory planning for MCT reduction in UAV-assisted WSN data collection. The MCT is affected by the flight time among GNs and the data collection time inside GN communication regions. Therefore, this thesis examined the relationship between the flight trajectory and the data collection trajectory.    

    This thesis proposed a trajectory planning method based on the Reference-Path Attractor (RPA) mechanism and the Boundary-Overlap Strategy (BO). The RPA mechanism uses a shortest geometric reference path to provide global directional guidance for local data collection trajectory planning. For single-node service, the UAV adjusts its trajectory inside each communication region according to the required data amount. For consecutive GNs with overlapping communication regions, BO allows service switching inside the overlap region and plans the overlapping GNs as a continuous service segment. Simulation results showed that the proposed method reduced the overall MCT compared with the benchmark methods under different data requirements. The results also showed that the proposed method maintained a shorter flight distance and reduced the data collection time. This result confirms the effectiveness of the joint consideration of the flight trajectory and the data collection trajectory.

    
\section{Future Work}
	Several directions can be further studied. First, the current study assumes a fixed UAV altitude and a fixed flight speed except during hovering. Future studies can include altitude and speed control to further reduce the MCT under practical UAV motion constraints. Second, this thesis focuses on a single-UAV data collection task. Future studies can extend the proposed method to multi-UAV scenarios to further improve mission efficiency in large-scale WSNs. Finally, future studies can consider the visiting order and the local trajectory planning process together to further improve the overall data collection performance.
}\end{thesis}
